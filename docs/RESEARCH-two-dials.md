# Two dials unlock every English conditional

English conditionals are not five separate "types" but **one system with two settings per clause**: a *reality* setting (open/real vs remote/unreal) and a *time* setting (past, present/always, future), plus one morphological rule, **"a past form with no past-time meaning = one step away from reality"**, which also explains *wish*, *if only*, *it's time*, *would rather*, *as if* and polite *could/would*. Moving a sentence from real to unreal is a **one-step backshift** (present → past, past → past perfect, *will* → *would* → *would have*), the same move learners already know from reported speech. This matters because corpus studies agree that the textbook zero/first/second/third/mixed scheme covers only **about 44–55% of real if-sentences**, while real (open) conditionals make up about **75%** of usage, the "first conditional" *if + present, will* is under 9%, and mixed conditionals are about 1.5% ([Jones & Waller 2011](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/1531/1/elt.ccp101.full_1531.pdf); [Gabrielatos](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/882)). For Thai teenagers the bottleneck is form, not concept: Thai marks conditions and counterfactuality with words (ถ้า, คง, ไปแล้ว, ป่านนี้), never on the verb, and the only Thai secondary-school study found that **64% of conditional errors fall on the two counterfactual types**, dominated by present simple and *will* where English needs backshift ([Katip & Gampper 2016](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/LEARN/article/view/102642)). The best-evidenced teaching route is a concept diagram (the reality × time grid) that learners verbalise, followed by meaning-driven tasks and corrective feedback, with Thai used to discuss meaning but not as word-for-word translation. This report gives the model, the corpus case, the decision rules, the C1–C2 extensions, the Thai error catalogue, the CEFR ladder, an original context bank for Bangkok girls aged 13–17, sensitivity rules, and a blueprint for turning it all into T.Chris's "Conditional Sentences" skill.

## Two dials and one rule generate every conditional form

### Reference grammars sort conditionals by reality and time, not by number

The "Type 0/1/2/3 + mixed" list is a teaching shortcut for four frequent verb pairings, not the organising principle of the grammar. Huddleston's *CGEL* tradition contrasts **open** conditionals ("If he comes tomorrow, we can settle the issue then") with **remote** ones, where the if-clause takes a preterite with "modal remoteness" meaning and the result takes the preterite of the auxiliary (*could*, *would*); in the remote version, *can* would be ungrammatical **[V]** ([Huddleston 1995](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/myl/huddleston1995.pdf)). Crucially, Huddleston's remote meaning covers both propositions assumed false and future events seen as unlikely, so **remote does not mean impossible** **[V]**. Quirk et al. use "open vs hypothetical" and separate direct from indirect conditions **[V2]** ([Revistas UVigo](https://revistas.uvigo.es/index.php/AFIAL/article/download/157/155)); Swan teaches "ordinary" vs "special" if-structures, and Celce-Murcia & Larsen-Freeman group them as factual, future/predictive and imaginative **[B]**. The second axis, **time**, is set separately for each clause. Sweetser adds a third dimension that matters at C1+: conditionals can work in the **content** domain (real cause), the **epistemic** domain (evidence → conclusion) or the **speech-act** domain (the condition is on saying something) **[V]** ([Sweetser 1990](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/from-etymology-to-pragmatics/conditionals/95A87911A3B230AE97C0354FC7677C7E)), and Dancygier & Sweetser show that the textbook tense restrictions are "largely relaxed" when a conditional is not predictive **[V]** ([Dancygier & Sweetser 2005](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/mental-spaces-in-grammar/future-and-present-forms-in-conditional-constructions/C129FB1FBD5615332C3578635E5E99A3)).

The compact model for the skill is therefore: **[connector] + condition clause + result clause, where each clause picks a time and the sentence picks a stance** **[D]**. The four textbook types are four cells. Zero = open + always; first = open + future with *will*; second = remote + present/future; third = remote + past; mixed = remote with different times in each clause. Gabrielatos adds that zero and first differ only in time reference (timeless vs future), not modality, and should be merged as "open conditionals" **[V]** ([Gabrielatos](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/882)).

**The single most important idea above the "type" level is that open vs remote is the speaker's stance, not a fact about the world** **[D]**. "If I win the scholarship, I'll study in Japan" treats winning as live; "If I won the scholarship, I'd study in Japan" treats the same event as a daydream. Both are grammatical and they make different claims about attitude. A weather-style percentage ("Type 2 = 50%") is therefore a teacher-made myth.

**Table 1. The reality × time grid (core diagram) [D; concept backed by Jones & Waller's real/unreal × time scheme, [V]]**

| | Past | Now / always | Future |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real / open** | If it rained, we stayed inside. (real past habit) | If you heat ice, it melts. / If you're hungry, eat something. | If it rains, we'll cancel. / …we might cancel. |
| **Unreal / remote** | If it had rained, we'd have cancelled. | If I had a car, I'd drive you. | If it rained tomorrow, we'd cancel. (less likely, imagined) |

Mixed conditionals are diagonal arrows between cells of the remote row: unreal past → unreal now ("If she had studied medicine, she'd be a doctor now") and unreal now/permanent → unreal past ("If he were more careful, he wouldn't have crashed") **[D]**.

### "Past form = distance from reality" unifies a dozen rules

English past-tense morphology has three uses: past time ("They arrived yesterday"), backshift in reported speech ("Max said he loved her"), and **modal remoteness** ("If you started tomorrow, you'd have enough time"), a term Huddleston adapts from Lyons **[V]** ([Huddleston 1995](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/myl/huddleston1995.pdf)). He treats all three as one tense, rejects the "past subjunctive" label (the only survivor is irrealis *were*), and shows that *would* has all three uses too (past habit, backshift, remoteness), which is why *would/could/might* are best taught as the remote forms of *will/can/may* **[V]**. Semanticists call the conditional past a "fake past": Iatridou (2000) argued it is modal, excluding the actual world rather than the present time, as summarised by Mackay **[V2]** ([Mackay 2015](https://semprag.org/index.php/sp/article/download/sp.8.12/pdf_8_12a)). Dancygier & Sweetser cite Fleischman (1989) for a widespread cross-linguistic link between past marking and distanced stance **[V2]** ([D&S, Tense and epistemic distance](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/mental-spaces-in-grammar/tense-epistemic-distance-and-embedded-spaces/90590818462360890BCD3EBF9113E088)). Michael Lewis's *The English Verb* (1986) frames the same idea for teachers as "remoteness" operating on temporal, hypothetical and social levels **[V2]** ([Diacronia paper on *would*](https://www.diacronia.ro/indexing/details/A27522/pdf)). The British Council's C1 "Unreal time" page states the classroom version directly: the tense change "suggests a distance from reality" **[V]** ([BC Unreal time](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/comment/204043)), and EF groups *if, supposing, if only, what if, I'd rather, it's time* as one "unreal past" family **[V]** ([EF](https://www.ef.co.uk/english-resources/english-grammar/unreal-past/)). Langacker's "distal" past and Leech's "hypothetical past" and "attitudinal past" make the same point **[B]**.

**Table 2. The distance family: one rule, eight structures [examples D; principle V]**

| Structure | Example | What the "past" form signals |
|---|---|---|
| Remote conditional | If I had a car, I'd drive you. | unreal or unlikely present/future |
| *wish / if only* | I wish I had a car. / If only I'd revised! | wanted ≠ real |
| *it's (high/about) time* + past | It's time we left. | should already be happening (vs neutral "It's time to go") |
| *would rather* + another subject | I'd rather you didn't tell Mum. | preferred ≠ real (yet) |
| *suppose / supposing / what if / imagine* | Suppose you lost your phone — what would you do? | imagined scenario (present tense = open: "Suppose she says no — what will you do?") |
| *as if / as though* | She talks as if she owned the school. | appearance ≠ reality |
| Polite past | I was wondering if you could help me. | social distance = softer |
| Modal past | Could you open the window? / I would say… | less direct, less certain |

Two cautions keep the rule honest. First, remote forms do not make an event impossible: "If you came on Friday, we could go together" is a polite suggestion about a perfectly possible event **[D]**. Second, a past simple in an if-clause is genuinely ambiguous between real past ("If it rained, we stayed inside" = whenever it rained) and remote present/future ("If it rained, we'd stay inside"); the result clause usually decides **[V]** ([D&S](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/mental-spaces-in-grammar/tense-epistemic-distance-and-embedded-spaces/90590818462360890BCD3EBF9113E088)). About **one third of past-tense if-clauses in Gabrielatos's BNC sample referred to real past time**, a use coursebooks treat as a special case **[V]** ([Gabrielatos](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/882)).

### One step back turns real into unreal

Moving an open conditional into the remote row moves **each clause one step into the past**, exactly as in reported speech; when a form is already past, the next step back is made with the perfect, because English has no "past of the past" inflection **[D, systematised from the cited principle]**. The British Council states the rule as present → past and past → past perfect for unreal situations **[V]** ([BC Unreal time](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/comment/204043)), and Huddleston shows the same preterite is required in backshifted reports ("He said you could (*can) leave") and remote results **[V]**. This is also why the third conditional needs *had* + V3 and *would have* + V3: the past slot is already used up for remoteness, so past *time* needs a second layer, the perfect **[D]**.

**Table 3. The step-back ladder [D]**

| Real / open form | One step back (remote now/future) | Two steps back (remote past) |
|---|---|---|
| present simple *goes* | past simple *went* | past perfect *had gone* |
| present continuous *is going* | past continuous *was going* | past perfect continuous *had been going* |
| present perfect *has gone* | past perfect *had gone* | past perfect (no further step) |
| *will go* | *would go* | *would have gone* |
| *can go* | *could go* | *could have gone* |
| *may go* | *might go* | *might have gone* |
| *must / have to go* | *would have to go* | *would have had to go* |

A worked set on one situation: "If it rains, the match will be cancelled" → "If it rained, the match would be cancelled" → "If it had rained, the match would have been cancelled" **[D]**. Two teacher cautions: the ladder is a mental model, not a rule to apply mechanically, because many open conditionals are already about the past ("If she said that, she was joking") and do not shift unless the meaning becomes contrary-to-fact; and not every modal has a same-meaning remote twin (*must* → *would have to*; *should* stays *should* or becomes *should have*) **[D]**. Reported-speech backshift is temporal distance while conditional backshift is modal distance: same forms, different reason **[D]**. The Thai mnemonic proposed by the pedagogy researcher captures this: **ถอยหลังหนึ่งก้าว = ถอยห่างจากความจริง** ("step back one tense = step away from reality") **[D]**.

### The full remote grid generates every mixed form

**Table 4. Remote conditionals: condition time × result time [D; BC and Wikipedia examples confirm both mixed directions, V]**

| Condition ↓ / Result → | Present (now) | Future | Past |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Present/permanent** (past simple, past continuous, *were*) | If I **were** taller, I'**d play** basketball. | If I **knew** her number, I'**d call** her tomorrow. | If she **weren't** so organised, she'**d have missed** the deadline. |
| **Future** (past simple, *were to*, *should*) | (rare) | If you **left** at six, you'**d arrive** by noon. / If you **were to** resign, who **would** replace you? | — |
| **Past** (past perfect) | If she **had studied** medicine, she **would be** a doctor now. | If you **had booked** last week, we'**d be flying** tomorrow. | If I **had seen** the sign, I **wouldn't have parked** there. |

Continuous and perfect-continuous aspects slot in normally: "If it wasn't raining, we'd be playing outside"; "If she hadn't been texting, she wouldn't have walked into the door" **[D]**. The British Council teaches both mixed directions at B1–B2 **[V]** ([BC Third and mixed](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/conditionals-third-mixed)), and test-english gives "If I spoke German, I would have understood them" and "If I had won the lottery, I would be rich" **[V]** ([test-english B2](https://test-english.com/explanation/b2/all-conditionals-mixed-conditionals-alternatives-if-inversion/)). Because mixed forms are only about 1.5% of real use, Gabrielatos judges teaching them as a separate "type" educationally unsound **[V]**; the grid lets them fall out naturally. The student diagnostic for any clause is two questions: **"When?"** (try a time adverb: *now / tomorrow / yesterday*) and **"Real or imagined?"**, then build the verb **[D]**.

### *Were* vs *was* and the limits of counterfactuality

Irrealis *were* is the formal and exam-safe choice; *was* is normal in speech. In American corpora *if I was* has overtaken *if I were* (COCA 2015–19: **10.42 vs 9.38 per million**; COHA since 2010: 14.24 vs 13.96), but *if I were you* still leads (0.31 vs 0.20 per million) **[V]** ([Clark, BYU *Schwa* 2022](https://schwa.byu.edu/files/2022/02/If-It-Was-the-Subjunctive-Would-It-Be-Wrong.pdf)). *CGEL* calls this *were* "irrealis", and Pullum treats *if I was you* as an accepted alternative **[V2]** ([Language Log](https://languagelog.ldc.upenn.edu/nll/?p=3038)); the *Chicago Manual of Style* labels it colloquial **[V]** ([CMOS](https://www.chicagomanualofstyle.org/qanda/data/faq/topics/Usage/faq0015.html)). *Were* is **obligatory in inversion** ("Were I…", never "Was I…") **[V]** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_conditional_sentences)). And *was* is **required** when the if-clause is a real past possibility: "If I was rude last night, I apologise"; *were* there would be a hypercorrection **[V]** ([Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.com/frequently-asked-questions/if-i-were-or-was/)). Finally, "third conditional = it didn't happen" is a very strong tendency rather than a logical entailment: the classic counter-example (attributed to Anderson 1951) is a doctor reasoning that if the patient had taken arsenic he would show exactly the symptoms he does show, as an argument that he *did* take it **[B]**.

## Textbook types cover under half of real if-sentences

Every corpus study found tells the same story. **Jones & Waller analysed 192 conditionals from a random BNC sample** using Maule's real/unreal × past/non-past categories **[V]** ([Jones & Waller 2011](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/1531/1/elt.ccp101.full_1531.pdf)):

**Table 5. Real usage in the BNC (Jones & Waller 2011) [V]**

| Category | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| Real non-past | 131 | 68.2% (spread over 24 verb-form patterns) |
| Real past | 14 | 7.3% |
| Unreal non-past | 38 | 19.8% |
| Unreal past | 9 | 4.7% |

| Most frequent patterns | Count | Share |
|---|---|---|
| *if* + present simple, present simple | 61 | 31.8% |
| *if* + past simple, *would* | 21 | 10.9% |
| *if* + present simple, *will* ("first conditional") | 17 | 8.9% |
| *if* + past simple, past simple (real past) | 11 | 5.7% |
| *if* + present simple, *may* | 10 | 5.2% |
| *if* + present simple, *can* | 8 | 4.2% |

The four exact textbook patterns covered **105 of 192 (about 55%)**, and the classic third conditional pattern appeared only 6 times **[V]**. Earlier studies reported by Jones & Waller agree: Maule (1988) found 68 real and 32 unreal conditionals in 100 TV samples, with only 7 textbook *if + present, will* sentences; Fulcher (1991) found *if + present simple* with a present, present modal or imperative result in **65.55% of 299** written conditionals; and Willis (1990) found hypothetical *would* most often with no if-clause at all **[V2]**. Gabrielatos's sample of 1,000 written BNC if-sentences found that even the combined typology of ten coursebooks covered only **about 44%**, with mixed conditionals at **1.5%**; in a later 781-instance sample, the common-ground typology of advanced coursebooks covered 27.8%, and everything every coursebook said still covered only 72.5% **[V]** ([Gabrielatos](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/882); [Gabrielatos 2011](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/50096)). Winter & Le Foll's comparison of 620 if-conditionals each from French EFL textbooks, French learner English, web English and the BNC found the typology covers "much less than half" of reference-corpus if-sentences, and only **57% even inside the textbooks themselves** **[V]** ([Winter & Le Foll 2022](https://www.benjamins.com/catalog/ijlcr.20021.win)).

Learner corpora show what this does to students. In Gabrielatos's ICLE vs BNC comparison, learners overused conditionals whose main clause expresses likelihood (*will/would* predictions) and underused real-past if-clauses and modality inside the if-clause **[V]** ([CORE record](https://core.ac.uk/works/478229)). Hasselgård found Norwegian advanced learners used if/unless-conditionals **more than three times as often** as British student writers (124.9 vs 39.4 per 100,000 words), with more hypotheticals (21.6% vs 12.2%) and fewer argument-building **epistemic** conditionals (21.6% vs 34.8%) **[V]** ([Hasselgård 2016](https://publicera.kb.se/njes/article/download/34240/27223/78391)). Conditionals also carry a measurably higher "modal load" than other constructions, including in the if-clause itself **[V]** ([Gabrielatos 2010 PhD](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/eprint/49616/)).

In speech, the picture shifts further from the textbook. **Pragmaticalised conditionals, used for stance, politeness and engagement rather than cause and effect, outnumber prototypical ones in British conversation** **[V]** ([Lastres-López 2020](https://minerva.usc.gal/entities/publication/f2998136-58b8-40de-94f1-1a4dd691da7f)). Standalone ("insubordinate") if-clauses with no main clause are the most frequent insubordination type in English, and **polite requests are their top function**, ahead of wishes and suggestions **[V]** ([Lastres-López 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/english-today/article/subordination-and-insubordination-in-contemporary-spoken-english/F9CA119E2D40363FA7820A95B560165A); [Lastres-López 2017](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/documents/college-artslaw/corpus/conference-archives/2017/general/paper193.pdf)). In the Spoken BNC2014 *if* is among the 50 most frequent word forms, and if-clauses and main clauses are often split across speakers' turns **[V]** ([Jones & Waller reply to Burton](https://knowledge.lancashire.ac.uk/id/eprint/40706/1/40706%20If%20we%20could%20suggest%20main%20article%20accepted.pdf)).

The implication for the skill is a **corpus-honest priority order** **[D]**: (1) *if + present, present / imperative / modal* for instructions, rules and habits; (2) *if + present, will / can / may / might / should* for predictions, offers and warnings; (3) *if + past, would / could / might* for imagining, advice and politeness; (4) *if + past, past* for real past habits and stories; (5) third and mixed conditionals, which are rare but error-prone and exam-relevant. Jones & Waller recommend labels such as "real/unreal + time" instead of "first/second", more time on real conditionals, modals beyond *will/would*, conditionals without *if*, function-based teaching, and learner-built "pedagogic corpora" **[V]**. The four type names should survive only as nicknames, because Thai school tests and Gateway still use them **[D]**.

## Any modal, any tense and a dozen connectors fill the two slots

### The result clause takes any modal; stance picks its form

*Will* and *would* are defaults, not requirements. A first-conditional result can be an imperative, another modal, or a deduction, and a second-conditional *would* can be replaced by *could, should, might*; the result can be a statement, question or command **[V]** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_conditional_sentences)). Jones & Waller suggest, for example, a first-aid lesson built on *if + can't, must* **[V]**. The modal carries its own meaning; the open/remote setting then selects its present or past form **[D]**.

**Table 6. Result-clause options [D]**

| Open: "If you finish early, …" | Meaning | Remote: "If I had more time, …" | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| …you'll get a bonus. | confident prediction | …I'd learn Korean. | imagined certain result / intention |
| …you may / might get a bonus. | possibility | …I could learn Korean. | imagined ability ("would be able to") |
| …you can leave. | permission | …I might learn Korean. | imagined possibility |
| …you should check your answers. | advice | …I should learn Korean. | imagined advice |
| …you must hand it in. | obligation | (*must* not used; say *would have to*) | — |
| …hand it in. | instruction | Remote past: "If she had trained harder, she would / could / might have won." | certain / able / possible |
| …can you help Ploy? | request | Remote question: "What would you do if you found a wallet?" | — |

Students overuse *would* for everything; "If I had a car, I could drive you" (ability) and "I would drive you" (willingness) say different things **[D]**. Avoid *must/can/will/may* in remote results; Huddleston's starred *can* shows the mismatch is ungrammatical **[V]**.

### The if-clause takes whatever tense fits, with one restriction

In open conditionals the if-clause uses any tense or aspect that fits its time and meaning: present perfect for a completed prerequisite ("If you've finished, you can go"), present continuous for something in progress ("If you're going to the shop, can you get some milk?"), *be going to* for a present intention, past simple or continuous for real past facts ("If she told you that, she was lying"), and modals ("If you can't come, let me know"; "If you should see her, say hello") **[D; BC confirms present perfect and present-for-future, V]** ([BC Verbs in time and if clauses](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/comment/143083)). The one real restriction is that **future time in predictive conditionals is shown with a present form, not *will***, the same rule as time clauses with *when, after, until, as soon as* **[V]**. The reason is meaning: the if-clause sets up an assumed scenario rather than predicting one **[B, Dancygier 1998 / D&S]**. Generic conditionals can have present in both clauses ("He gets angry if I leave the house") **[V]** ([D&S ch. 4](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/mental-spaces-in-grammar/future-and-present-forms-in-conditional-constructions/C129FB1FBD5615332C3578635E5E99A3)).

### *Will* and *would* after *if* are fine when they carry modal meaning

"Never *will/would* after *if*" is an overstatement. *Will* in an if-clause is standard when it means **willingness** ("I will be very happy if you will come to my party", which can sound polite or irritated) **[V]** ([BC](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/comment/143083)), **stressed insistence** ("If you *will* keep all the windows shut, of course you'll get headaches", where *will* cannot contract) **[V]**, or when the if-event **follows** the main event ("If it would make Bill happy, I would give him the money") **[V]** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_conditional_sentences)). Polite *would* in requests ("If you would wait a moment, please…") is attested from Palmer (1990) **[V2]** ([Diacronia](https://www.diacronia.ro/indexing/details/A27522/pdf)), and the metalinguistic hedge "if you will" ("the modern-day Socrates, if you will") is a third legitimate use **[V, teacher source]** ([Kate Taylor](https://englishlearninghubwithkatetaylor.webador.ie/grammar-with-kate/1884383_to-will-or-not-to-will-in-if-clauses)).

*If I would have known* is different: it is widespread native American speech but stigmatised in writing. In a small, mainly Minnesota sample, **41% of past-counterfactual if-clauses (12/29) and 52% of *wish*-clauses (13/25) used *would have***; most non-ESL American informants left it uncorrected while most ESL professionals corrected it; and of 34 reference and teaching sources, most condemned or ignored it, with 12 explicitly prescribing the past perfect. The form dates from the 15th century, and ETS reportedly avoids testing it as an error **[V]** ([Ishihara 2003](https://journalpub.escholarship.org/ial/article/6886/galley/3985/download)). The skill should teach it for **recognition only** and mark it wrong in exam writing **[D]**.

The student decision rule **[D]**: if *will/would* could be replaced by "be willing to / agree to", or you are stressing someone's stubbornness, or the result comes before the condition, it is fine ("If you'll just sign here, we can start"; "If you won't listen, I can't help you"; "If it'll save time, I'll take a taxi"; "I'd be grateful if you would reply by Friday"). If *will* only means "in the future", drop it ("~~If it will rain~~ If it rains tomorrow, we'll cancel"). In speech, *'d* + V3 = *had*, *'d* + base verb = *would*, *'d better* = *had*: "If I'd known, I'd have come" contains both **[V]** ([Gymglish](https://gymglish.com/en/gymglish/english-grammar/contractions-would-vs-had)).

### Each connector adds one nuance, and several do not swap with *if*

**Table 7. Connector map [examples D; meanings sourced as marked]**

| Connector | Core meaning | Example | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| *if* | neutral condition | If Mum agrees, I'll come. | — |
| *when* | expected event **[V]** ([Grammarly](https://www.grammarly.com/commonly-confused-words/if-vs-when)) | When Mum gets home, I'll ask her. | In general truths *if ≈ when ≈ whenever*. |
| *unless* | except if; the one thing that stops the result | I'll walk unless it rains. | Fails when the *non*-event causes the result: "I'll be angry if the bus isn't on time" ≠ "*unless the bus is on time"; also odd with "If she wasn't so bossy…" **[V, Swan via Onestopenglish]** ([Onestopenglish](https://www.onestopenglish.com/ask-the-experts/grammar-unless-and-if/146350.article)). Never *unless … not*. |
| *in case* | precaution: do X now because Y might happen | Take an umbrella in case it rains. | ≠ *if* ("Take an umbrella if it rains" = only when it is raining). ### Non-predictive conditionals break the tense "rules" correctly

Many natural conditionals do not say "if P happens, Q happens", and teachers who miss this "correct" good English **[D]**. **Speech-act or "biscuit" conditionals** (after Austin) condition the *relevance* of saying something: the biscuits are on the sideboard whether or not you want them, and adding *then* destroys the reading **[V]** ([Swanson](https://counterexamples.github.io/research/Swanson,%20Subjunctive%20Biscuit%20and%20Stand-Off%20Conditionals.pdf)). **Epistemic conditionals** argue from evidence to conclusion ("If her bag's here, she's still at school"; "If she said that, she was lying"), allowing any tense pairing **[D]**. **Metalinguistic conditionals** comment on word choice ("I saw my ex-husband, if that's the right word for him", Dancygier & Sweetser 1996) **[V2]** ([Wikipedia, Conditional sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_sentence)), and *if you like* / *if you see what I mean* work as hedges that reduce commitment **[V]** ([Elder 2019](https://research-portal.uea.ac.uk/en/publications/metalinguistic-conditionals-and-the-role-of-explicit-content/)). *If* also has a concessive sense ("an interesting if untenable argument") and an "even perhaps" sense ("difficult if not impossible") **[V]** ([Merriam-Webster, *if*](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/if)). The rule for any marker or app checker **[D]**: **before correcting an if-sentence that breaks the textbook pattern, decide whether it is epistemic, speech-act, factual or real-past; if it is, the tense combination is probably correct.**

### Clause order and the comma follow information, not grammar

Either clause can come first without changing truth conditions **[V]** ([BC](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/b1-b2-grammar/conditionals-zero-first-second)). In academic writing the if-clause comes first about **61–64%** of the time and last about 33–36% **[V]** ([Hasselgård 2016](https://publicera.kb.se/njes/article/download/34240/27223/78391)). A fronted if-clause sets the scene; a final one adds the condition as new information, a focus (*only if, unless*) or an afterthought ("We could go to Siam Paragon — if you're free") **[D, consistent with D&S framing]**. The comma is a strong convention, not a rule: comma after a fronted if-clause ([Purdue OWL](https://owl.purdue.edu/owl/general_writing/punctuation/commas/commas_after_introductions.html)) **[V]**, usually none before a final one unless it is a speech-act aside ("There's water in the fridge, if you're thirsty"), and always after an inverted condition ("Had I known, I would have called") **[D]**. *Then* is optional and emphatic, and is odd with *even if* and biscuit conditionals **[B, Iatridou 1994]**.

## Native control at C1–C2 means choosing form for stance

### Inversion uses only *should*, *were* and *had*

Standard modern English allows conditional inversion with only three auxiliaries: **should** (open, slightly less likely future: "Should you require further information, please contact the office"), **were** (remote present/future, including *were to*: "Were the government to raise taxes, spending would fall") and **had** (past counterfactual: "Had I known about the delay, I would have taken the train"). The British Council labels this C1, says it "makes the sentence more formal", and notes *should* makes a situation "a little less likely" and suits polite offers **[V]** ([BC Inversion and conditionals](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/c1-grammar/inversion-conditionals?page=0)). Negatives must be uncontracted with *not* after the subject ("Had she not…", never "Hadn't she…"); *CGEL* calls the process "subject-auxiliary conversion" and treats inverted conditionals as remote **[V2]** ([Grammarphobia](https://grammarphobia.com/blog/2019/07/conditional.html)). *Were* only, never *was*; fixed negatives are *were it not for* and *had it not been for* **[V]** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_conditional_sentences)). Mixed-time inversion exists ("Had she not left early, she would be here now") **[D]**. Literary *Could I but…* / *Did he but know…* are recognition-only C2 items **[B]**. The typical learner errors are keeping *if* ("*If had I known"), contracting the negative, and *Was I to…* **[D]**. Register advice: one or two inversions in an IELTS or CAE essay show range; *Should you have any questions…* is a standard email formula; in casual speech inversion sounds pompous, except *Had I known…*, which is common **[D]**.

### *Were to* and *should* tune likelihood

*If + were to* marks a hypothetical future as more remote or more formal ("If the plan were to fail, who would take responsibility?"); *were to have* + V3 is its past **[V]** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_conditional_sentences)). *If + should* marks an open but "by chance" condition and allows *will* or an imperative in the result ("If anyone should call while I'm out, take a message") **[V]**. *Were to* is awkward with states ("?If I were to know the answer") **[B]**, and mixing a remote *were to* clause with a *will* result is an error **[D]**.

### Implied and reduced conditionals carry meaning without *if*

At C1–C2 much conditional meaning travels without a full if-clause: imperatives ("Go eastwards a mile and you'll see it") and stand-alone consequents ("I would.") are attested **[V]** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_conditional_sentences)), and *if not for* is a recognised verbless form **[V]** ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/if)). The wider inventory below is standard description with the researchers' own level and register labels **[B/D]**.

### Hedging, legal drafting and reported speech

In academic writing, conditionals and *would* are core hedges ("If this is the case…", "These findings would suggest…", "Were this pattern to hold…"); native student writers use epistemic conditionals to build arguments at about **35%** of their conditionals **[V]** ([Hasselgård 2016](https://publicera.kb.se/njes/article/download/34240/27223/78391)). Contracts use *provided that, in the event that, subject to, unless otherwise stated*, though drafting authority Ken Adams calls *provided that* imprecise and recommends avoiding it **[V]** ([Adams](https://www.adamsdrafting.com/revisiting-provided-that/)). Journalism frames scenarios with *If current trends continue…*, *Barring a last-minute deal…*, *Should talks fail…* **[D]**. In reported speech, first conditionals backshift ("If it rains, we'll cancel" → she said that if it rained, they would cancel) **[V]** ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_conditional_sentences)), while second and third conditionals normally stay unchanged; over-backshifting a second conditional into a third changes the meaning **[B/D]**. Natives also shorten constantly: "I'd help you if I could", "Come early if you can", "If so / If not" **[D]**.

### Pragmatics: the same grammar threatens, flatters and blames

Conditionals are a main English tool for indirectness. A contrast set shows the C2 skill of choosing form for interpersonal effect: "If you need anything, call me" (neutral) / "Should you need anything, call me" (formal, less likely) / "If you needed anything, you could call me" (tentative, distant) **[D]**. Second-person *will* + a negative consequence reads as a threat ("If you touch my phone again, you'll regret it"); a third or mixed conditional points a finger ("If you'd listened to me, we wouldn't be lost"), and "It might have been better if we'd checked the map" softens it; *If only I'd…* expresses regret; "If he's a genius, I'm Einstein" is sarcasm **[D]**. *Should*-inversion and *should* in if-clauses soften offers and requests **[V]** ([BC](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/grammar/c1-grammar/inversion-conditionals?page=0)); *wish/if only + would* about other people often shows annoyance **[V]** ([BC Unreal time](https://learnenglish.britishcouncil.org/comment/204043)). Thai learners already use conditionals this way in their L1: a Chulalongkorn study of Thai novels found conditionals serving **23 speech intentions**, including warning, threatening, persuading, requesting, teasing and apologising **[V]** ([Niamboobpha 2001](https://cuir.car.chula.ac.th/handle/123456789/66195)), which gives the skill a ready cultural bridge.

## Instructions, requests and "what if" talk dominate real use

The genres teenagers actually read run on the most frequent pattern, *if + present, imperative/modal*. The EU medicine-leaflet template has standard headings beginning *If* (taking too much, forgetting a dose, stopping) **[V]** ([EMA QRD template](https://epi.ema.europa.eu/qrdtemplate/lists/200000029659/list.html)); Microsoft's error-message guidelines are themselves written as if-rules **[V]** ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/win32/debug/error-message-guidelines)); Shopee Singapore's help centre organises topics as *What should I do if…?* and *What happens if…?* questions **[V]** ([Shopee SG FAQ](https://help.shopee.sg/portal/4/article/76703-[FAQ]-About-Orders-and-Cancellations)); Singapore's aviation-security guidance uses many implied conditionals with no *if* **[V]** ([CAAS](https://caas.gov.sg/public-passengers/before-your-flight/passenger-public-security)); and coding curricula teach *if / else if / else* through chores and branching stories **[V]** ([Flocabulary](https://www.flocabulary.com/lesson-plan-coding-conditionals/); [Code.org](https://studio.code.org/s/coursed-2022/lessons/12?lang=es-ES)). One Bangkok Post *Learning* article on PM2.5 contained no if-sentences at all, expressing risk through thresholds instead, a reminder that conditions often hide in *when* and threshold wording (one article only, so anecdotal) **[V]** ([Bangkok Post Learning](https://www.bangkokpost.com/learning/easy/2733841/capitals-pm2-5-levels-soar)). Unreal conditionals cluster instead in opinion, storytelling, sports punditry and fandom "what if" talk **[D]**.

## Thai marks conditions with words, so learners skip the verb change

### How Thai builds a condition

Thai marks a condition lexically and never on the verb **[V]**. The basic frame is **ถ้า P, (subject) ก็ Q**, where ก็ is an optional resumptive "then" that sits after an overt subject and before จะ (ก็จะ, never จะก็); the condition may also follow the result, and then ก็ usually drops ([elon.io: Condition](https://elon.io/grammar/thai/complex-clauses/condition), a learner-grammar site consistent with the reference grammars). **หาก / หากว่า** are formal equivalents used in notices and contracts: หากพบข้อมูลไม่ถูกต้อง กรุณาแจ้งเจ้าหน้าที่ภายในเจ็ดวัน = "If you find incorrect information, please inform an officer within seven days" **[V]**. Thai can also mark a condition with no ถ้า at all, by juxtaposition plus ก็ (กดปุ่มนี้ ไฟก็จะดับ = "Press this button and the light goes off") or by paired negatives (ไม่ลองก็ไม่รู้ = "(If you) don't try, (you) won't know") **[V]** ([elon.io](https://elon.io/grammar/thai/complex-clauses/condition); [Savetamalya 2016](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/abc/article/view/168101)), which maps neatly onto English imperative + *and* and *or*.

**จะ is an irrealis marker, not a future tense.** It marks events as not (yet) actualised across possibility, probability, volition, future, **conditions**, habituality, deduction and purpose **[V]** ([Soithuhram 2010, Chulalongkorn PhD](https://cuir.car.chula.ac.th/dspace/handle/123456789/32786); [Soithuhram, SWU Humanities Journal](https://ejournals.swu.ac.th/index.php/hm/article/view/3056)). One Thai form therefore covers the semantic space of both *will* and *would*, and it can appear in both clauses. Counterfactuals use **ถ้า P, (ก็)คง Q**, with the unreal reading coming from context, time anchors (ตอนนั้น "at that time", วันนั้น "that day"), comparatives (เร็วกว่านี้ "faster than this") and the conjectural modal คง; negation is คงไม่ (not ไม่คง) **[V]** ([elon.io: Counterfactual](https://elon.io/grammar/thai/complex-clauses/counterfactual)). | ในกรณีที่ | in the case that | *in the event that*, *if* | Means *if*-type contingency, so it pushes learners to overuse *in case* (E10). |
| ตราบใดที่ | as long as | *as long as, provided (that)* | Clean 1:1 mapping. |
| เว้นแต่, นอกจาก(ว่า) | except (that), unless | *unless* | Also "except", which feeds confusion. |
| ไม่งั้น, ไม่อย่างนั้น, มิฉะนั้น | if not so / otherwise | *otherwise, or (else)* | มิฉะนั้น is formal. |
| …ก็… | result linker | *then*, Ø | Source of *If…, so…* (E13). |
| …จะ… | irrealis / prospective | *will, would, 'd* | One form for both *will* and *would*. |
| คง(จะ), น่าจะ | probably / must be | *would (probably), might, must have* | Marks hypothetical results; epistemic, not counterfactual. |
| ป่านนี้ | by now (this late) | *would … by now* | Strong mixed-conditional cue: ถ้าออกเร็ว ป่านนี้ก็ถึงแล้ว = "If we'd left early, we'd be there by now." |
| ตอนนั้น, วันนั้น | at that time / that day | past time anchor | Thai uses these where English uses *had* + V3 / *would have*. |
| …ไปแล้ว | completed, "already" | *would have* + V3 | ถ้า…ก็คง…ไปแล้ว ≈ third-conditional result. |
| แม้(ว่า), ถึงแม้ | even if / even though | *even if, even though, although* | One family for both meanings (E11). |
| เมื่อ | when | *when* | ถ้า/เมื่อ overlap in generic statements (E12). |

The key consequence **[D]**: one Thai template, **ถ้า P (ก็)(จะ/คง) Q**, maps onto all of English zero, first, second, third and mixed conditionals, so a learner thinking in Thai has **no formal trigger for backshift**. Thai speakers have the counterfactual concept; they express it with adverbs, comparatives and คง. That predicts errors of form, not of concept.

### What Thai learners actually get wrong

The closest study to SWEP students is **Katip & Gampper (2016)**: Grade 12 (M6) students in a Bangkok public-school integrated English programme, 68 on a gap-fill and 20 on a spoken task **[V]** ([LEARN Journal](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/LEARN/article/view/102642); [PDF](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/LEARN/article/download/102642/82189/259714)).

In speech, **future *will* was the top error (27.0% of tokens)**, then subject–verb agreement (17.71%), and **no student produced *would have* + past participle at all** **[V]**. The authors attribute this to developmental carry-over, cumulative complexity, *will*-overuse and decontextualised test items, and do not invoke L1 transfer **[V]**. Other evidence converges on the same two weak points. Suraprajit found Thai learners did best on zero conditionals, used first conditionals most in writing, rated the **second conditional hardest**, and often used the progressive in zero-conditional if-clauses **[V]** ([Suraprajit 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ibn/eltjnl/v15y2022i8p23.html)). Pojprasat's oral test of 32 Thai English majors found **only 7 passed**; errors were verb forms mismatched to meaning, attributed to negative Thai transfer, and **reported use of "mental pictures" significantly distinguished successful learners (p = 0.029)** **[V]** ([Pojprasat 2024](https://murex.mahidol.ac.th/en/publications/successful-thai-efl-students-and-their-language-learning-strategi/)). On *wish*-clauses, which share the backshift mechanism, intermediate Chulalongkorn first-years scored **6.67–13.33% on wish-past** vs 60–67% for advanced learners, failed to backshift about 34–36% of the time, and produced forms such as *I wish he will lend me his car*; the authors blame L1 transfer and the absence of *could have* + V3 from the high-school textbooks they reviewed **[V]** ([Suteerapongsit & Pongpairoj 2020](https://so04.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/jsel/article/view/242277)). Taiwanese senior-high students (also a tenseless L1) found present counterfactuals harder than past ones on judgement tasks and mainly omitted backshift and *have*, but that author concluded L1 transfer "seems to exert little influence" **[V]** ([Chang 2004](https://etds.lib.ntnu.edu.tw/thesis/detail/b720b254ae1daa5e7425db592abf4ee9)). The sources therefore disagree on cause; both transfer and structural complexity are probably at work **[D]**. Thai learners of Korean also used ถ้า and แม้ interchangeably, supporting the predicted *if / even if / even though* confusion **[V]** ([Lapruengroeng & Sinaroj 2026](https://so05.tci-thaijo.org/index.php/RRBR/article/view/285764)).

### The concept is not the bottleneck; working memory and dual meaning are

Counterfactuals carry a **dual meaning**, the supposition plus the implied opposite fact, and in offline tasks only about **50–67%** of people in Thompson & Byrne (2002) inferred the opposite facts, with counterfactual contexts reducing sensitivity to inconsistencies especially for **low-working-memory readers** **[V2]** ([Kulakova & Nieuwland 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4959139)). Counterfactual reasoning develops in early childhood (around 75% accuracy on simple scenarios at about 3.5 years) **[V]** ([Perner, Sprung & Steinkogler 2004](https://kris.kl.ac.at/en/publications/counterfactual-conditionals-and-false-belief-a-developmental-diss/)), and English-L1 children's production accuracy falls as complexity rises from Type I to III **[V]** ([Badger et al. 2020](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publications/1102466/modal)). A review notes that Chinese, which lacks subjunctive forms, leaves counterfactual premises to context, which may affect children's interpretation **[V]** ([Cambridge repository review](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/handle/1810/382574)); the same logic plausibly applies to Thai **[D]**. So for 13–17-year-olds **[D]**: anchor meaning first with Thai bridges and visual timelines, keep early tasks to one clause pair with explicit reality labels (TRUE / NOT TRUE / NOT TRUE IN THE PAST), add mixed time frames only at B2, and use low-stakes personal "what if" tasks to lower avoidance.

## Concept diagrams and meaning-driven tasks have the best evidence

### What the research supports

No study found shows one method clearly beating others for conditionals specifically, so the skill should state its teaching choices as evidence-informed rather than proven. The strongest evidence for **Concept-Based Language Instruction (CBLI)**, which materialises one concept in a diagram (a SCOBA card) and has learners verbalise how they use it, comes from closely related tense-aspect work **[E]**. Benhamlaoui & Gánem-Gutiérrez taught Algerian intermediate learners simple past, past continuous and present perfect with 50 diagram cards plus verbalisation (n = 25) versus PPP (n = 20): metalinguistic knowledge rose from 17.5 to 34.5 (post) and 29 (delayed), ηp² = .55, with no significant PPP change and group differences of about d ≈ 1.4; language-in-use gains appeared only at the three-week delayed test (35.2 → 49; d = 1.07) **[V]** ([Benhamlaoui & Gánem-Gutiérrez](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/32250/1/A%20Classroom%20Application%20of%20CBLI%20January%202022%20accepted%20030222.pdf)). Kissling's CBLI on Spanish aspect made novices use forms "more like advanced learners" **[V]** ([Kissling](https://jalda.azaruniv.ac.ir/article_14665.html)). On conditionals directly, a Chilean MA thesis comparing rule-based and Cognitive Grammar teaching for 36 university students found **similar global scores** **[V]** ([Azócar Plaza 2018](https://repositorio.uchile.cl/handle/2250/171009?show=full)), and Ivy Wong at York has a 2025 chapter on a concept-driven approach to English conditionals whose findings were inaccessible **[V, title only]** ([York](https://www.york.ac.uk/education/our-staff/academic/ivy-wong)).

For the hardest form, the past counterfactual, two studies help. **Focused communication tasks** (where the form is needed to reach a meaning goal) equalled contextualised practice on explicit knowledge but **outperformed it on implicit knowledge** **[E, V]** ([Broszkiewicz 2011](https://dspacetest.amu.edu.pl/items/81e18fdf-6655-406d-b5c1-718316df4d1c)). Text reconstruction plus written corrective feedback lifted 94 Turkish B1+ students' accuracy as follows **[E, V]** ([Uludağ 2025](https://eejpl.vnu.edu.ua/index.php/eejpl/article/download/950/398/2003)):

| Group | Pretest | Immediate | Delayed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct WCF | 23.45% | 67.34% | 61.78% |
| Indirect (metalinguistic) WCF | 24.18% | 58.92% | 59.45% |
| Control | 22.87% | 45.23% | 47.12% |

Direct feedback won immediately (d = .71) but the gap closed by the delayed test, where the indirect group held steady; the paper is internally inconsistent about the delay interval. Textual input enhancement beat explicit instruction alone for the passive with Iranian learners **[E, V]** ([DOAJ 2012](https://doaj.org/article/bc59d42e026a4c67b6a0ac7f1178f125)), but no conditional-specific enhancement study was found. In Thailand, scaffolded instruction gave 27 English majors significant gains **[E, V]** ([Issues in Language Studies 2024](https://publisher.unimas.my/ojs/index.php/ILS/article/view/5712)), and Pojprasat's visualisation finding (p = 0.029) is the only Thai-specific strategy evidence **[V]**. Learners score better on comprehension than production (5.95 vs 5.07, p = .049), so meaning and form should be assessed separately **[V]** ([Jasim, Univ. of Mosul](https://iasj.rdd.edu.iq/journals/uploads/2025/01/06/5e729a212c4a83916f44878979206783.pdf)). A 2026 review of 24 AI-chatbot speaking studies reports gains and lower anxiety but recommends chatbots only as a complement to teacher feedback **[V]** ([ETERNAL 2026](https://diajeng.lldikti6.id/articles/18784)). No study applied VanPatten-style processing instruction to conditionals; it remains an untested idea **[P]**.

The recipe that follows **[C/D]**: one coherent concept (distance from reality) → a materialised diagram (the grid) → learner verbalisation alone and in pairs, in Thai at B1 → meaning-driven tasks → immediate correction → a delayed re-check two to four weeks later.

| Meaning | Thai anchor | Example / note |

Popular Thai explainers describe zero as ใช้กับสิ่งที่เป็นความจริงเสมอ ("always true"), second as สิ่งที่ไม่เป็นความจริง / เหตุการณ์สมมติ, and third as เหตุการณ์สมมติที่เกิดขึ้นในอดีต, but **translate types 1–3 with the same ถ้า…จะ wording** and add a ความจริงคือ… ("the truth is…") line to show unreality **[V]** ([TruePlookpanya](https://www.trueplookpanya.com/knowledge/content/62689); [PREP](https://prepedu.com/th/blog/if-clause-type-3)). That illustrates both sides: Thai helps when it adds reality and time markers (คง, ไปแล้ว, ตอนนั้น, สมมติ, จริง ๆ แล้ว) and for CCQs such as เกิดขึ้นจริงไหม? ("Did it really happen?"); it hurts as word-for-word translation, because ถ้า…จะ produces *will* in every type **[D]**. Jones & Waller explicitly endorse L1 use for meaning discussion at any level **[V]**. The bilingual sequence: English model → Thai reality gloss → back to English with the form highlighted, fading Thai by stage 3–4 of the spiral; at C1, ask students to explain why Thai doesn't need tense here but English does, a CBLI-style verbalisation **[D]**.

### Activities and the spiral

Documented practitioner activities include the British Council's **conditional chain game** (each learner starts a new first conditional from the previous result) **[P, V]** ([TeachingEnglish](https://www.teachingenglish.org.uk/teaching-resources/teaching-primary/activities/level-3/conditional-chain-game)) and the US State Department's **superstitions** lesson (restate local superstitions as first conditionals, then mime them) **[P, V]** ([American English](https://americanenglish.state.gov/resources/superstitions)). The researchers' design-ready additions **[D]**: levelled chain stories (B1 first → B2 "If I won a prize…" → C1 butterfly chains ending in a mixed conditional); a **regret auction** where teams bid on correct vs incorrect regret sentences, targeting *would* in the if-clause; **dilemma tasks** ("You find a wallet with 5,000 baht outside 7-Eleven…") as the closest match to the focused-task evidence; *Would you rather* debates with two second-conditional reasons; role-plays (customer service, negotiating with parents, polite third-conditional complaints); a **find-the-condition** reading that includes *if*-less forms; an error hunt ("fix the time machine") seeded with E1–E17; a **probability ranking** of eight sentences from certain to impossible; Blooket or Kahoot items where at least a third are reality CCQs; AI-chatbot role-plays that end with a list of conditional errors and corrections, reviewed by the teacher; and songs used ethically, with students reporting a pattern from a song they choose without any lyrics reproduced in materials.

**Table 22. Spiral sequence [D]**

| Stage | Level | Content |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | B1 | Real now/always and real future with *will / can / might / should* / imperative; *unless*; *when* vs *if* |
| 2 | B1+ | Unreal now/future; *If I were you*; *wish* + past; introduce "one step back" |
| 3 | B2 | Unreal past; *wish / if only* + past perfect; link to *should have* |
| 4 | B2+ | Mixed both ways; *as long as / provided / otherwise / in case*; real-past conditionals |
| 5 | C1 | Inversion; *were to*; *but for / without / if it weren't for*; implied conditionals; polite *would* |
| 6 | C2 | Open vs remote for stance and politeness; argument-building conditionals; *will/would* for willingness and insistence |

Check **understanding** (reality CCQs, sort-to-grid, choose between two sentences for a situation, draw the timeline, explain in Thai) separately from **accuracy** (gap-fill, transformation, error correction, timed speaking), and report two scores, "Meaning" and "Form", in any app dashboard **[D]**.

**Table 23. Teacher-made myths and the correction [D, grounded in sources cited above]**

| Myth | Reality |
|---|---|
| Zero conditional = scientific facts only | Also habits, rules, instructions, personal generalisations ("If I drink coffee after 4, I can't sleep" is the British Council's own example) |
| First conditional = *will* + future | Open conditionals take any modal or imperative and any time; *will* is under 9% |
| Second = impossible / 50% | Remote = distanced; can be polite or tentative about possible events; the choice is stance |
| Second conditional past = past time | It is the distance past shared with *wish, it's time, I'd rather, Could you…?* |
| Third = regret | Any counterfactual past: relief, reasoning, criticism; counterfactuality is a strong tendency |
| Never *will/would* after *if* | Fine for willingness, insistence, politeness, result-before-condition, *if you will* |
| Always *were* | *Were* formal and required in inversion; *was* normal in speech, required for real past |
| *Unless* = *if not*, always | Not when the non-event causes the result, nor in most counterfactual regret |
| Each type has fixed forms | Clauses combine compositionally; any modal or aspect can fill the slots |
| Conditionals always have *if* | Inversion, imperative + *and/or*, *otherwise*, *but for*, *without*, stand-alone *would* |
| Comma always / never | Convention tied to clause order |
| Teach *wish* separately | It is the same distance mechanism |
| Form accuracy = understanding | Check meaning separately |

## Sensitivity rules exclude the monarchy entirely

Conditionals invite "If the king…" or "If I were prime minister…" prompts, so the skill needs an explicit block list for every generator, app and slide. Thailand's lèse-majesté law (Section 112) carries penalties of **up to 15 years per count**, reported sentences have exceeded 40 and 50 years, and in April 2025 a US lecturer at Naresuan University was arrested and had his visa revoked over an online blurb **[V]** ([Al Jazeera, 10 Apr 2025](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2025/4/10/us-academic-free-on-bail-in-thailand-after-detention-over-royal-defamation)). The rest of the list is the researchers' inference from legal context and general safeguarding practice, since no Thai EFL sensitivity guidance was found **[D]**.

**Table 25. Sensitivity rules [D except the legal basis]**

| Category | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Hard exclusions** | The monarchy and royal family in any form, including hypotheticals, role-swaps, royal-themed festivals and joke idioms that mention any king or queen; real Thai politicians, parties, coups and protests; religion as a joke (monks or temples as comic material); appearance-shaming ("If she were prettier…", weight, skin whitening); romance, dating, crushes, boyfriends; self-harm, eating disorders, drugs, gambling; violent crime as entertainment |
| **Handle with care** | Exam failure and TCAS stress (frame as recoverable); family money and debt (no "If your family were poor…"); friendship conflict (fictional names, no bullying of individuals); floods, haze and disasters (factual, no blame on regions or groups) |
| **Third-conditional regret** | Low-stakes regrets only (missed bus, forgotten umbrella, snack spending); never ask students to disclose real personal regrets publicly; offer fictional characters |
| **All-girls school** | Female or neutral protagonists in student roles; ambitious, team-based scenarios (doctor, engineer, athlete, entrepreneur); no gender stereotypes ("If I were a boy, I'd be good at maths"); imagery shows only girls or adults |

| **Threat examples** | Keep threats mild and everyday ("If you post that photo, I'm never talking to you again"); no violent threat idioms |

## Conclusion

The research changes the job of a conditionals skill from teaching five forms to teaching **two decisions and one move**. Once a student asks "real or imagined?" and "when?" for each clause and knows that a past form without past time means distance, the four types, mixed forms, *wish*, *it's time*, *would rather*, polite *could you* and even inversion become one family rather than a list of exceptions. That reframing matters doubly for Thai learners, because their L1 already expresses every one of these meanings with words like สมมติ, คง, ไปแล้ว and ป่านนี้; the skill's task is to make them notice that English moves the verb where Thai adds a word. The corpus evidence also turns a common priority upside down: real conditionals with modals, imperatives and fixed chunks deserve the most practice time because they are what Bangkok teenagers read in apps, help pages and videos, while the third conditional deserves the most *careful* time because it is rare, emotionally loaded and the single largest error source.

Two tensions remain for T.Chris to judge. Exams reward the tidy textbook forms (*were*, no *would* in the if-clause, *in case* only as precaution) while native usage is looser, so the skill must teach "exam-safe production, native-wide recognition" explicitly rather than calling acceptable variation an error. And the best teaching evidence (CBLI, focused tasks, feedback) comes mostly from adult or tense-aspect studies, so the skill should present its methods as evidence-informed designs and build in the separate Meaning and Form scores and delayed re-checks that would let T.Chris see whether they work with his own students.
